<How politically relevant would be our everyday conversations?> 

Classical studies on deliberation theory are concerned with identifying public discussions closer to high argumentative level, so that it could help to solve collective problems in the realm of traditional politics. Thus, much of the literature on the subject remains concentrated in electoral studies and political institutions, without paying enough attention to everyday conversations that also can contributes to the development of politicized discussions on relevant issues for democratic life. This essay explores the potential of everyday conversation for expanding social democratic practices and for the development of critical and reflexive subjects; which might involve a broader conception of politics, beyond the governmental and State spheres. To do so, I argue that we need to analyze firstly how everyday talks define themselves, to understand their contribution in the relational construction of the subjects as interlocutors and the ways of political agency they recall.

Traditional deliberative theory distinguishes everyday conversations from rational argumentative exchanges. The father of this theory, Habermas (1987), articulates two levels of discursive practices: the first corresponds to a ""naive communicative practice"" with an implicit or latent problematization degree; the second concerns to a reflexive use of language that aims at a mutual understanding. It is expected the latter should be able to produce political results toward a decision-making process regarding public problems. Thus, the research in the area of deliberation focus on evaluating the quality of argumentative discussions in which individuals can contribute to the democratic political mechanism within formal political arenas. Most research on conversational exchanges aims to evaluate the production, presentation and exchange of arguments and justifications in more common-life spaces, as well as the general performance of rational principles, that is, the argumentative-deliberative potential of these practices. This normative approach has been criticized for being based on highly demanding principles, such as the capacities demanded from the speakers in everyday conversations.

On the other hand, still inside deliberative theory, the more recent perspective of the deliberative system (MANSBRIDGE, 2012) focus on practices and interconnected flows through different arenas in society, where civic conversation and spontaneous communicative practices gain attention. In this approach, very demanding normative criteria seems to be not always appropriate for the analysis of everyday conversations. That statement highlights a notion of conversations as general interactions that mix political issues and events related to personal experience, with little argumentative structure and absence of rules that could guide the search for reciprocal justification. These characteristics lead us to think of possibilities of studying the conversations from their real situation of interaction, and not only as part of the deliberative and formally political framework in which they are embedded. 

In our view, conversation allows us to recognize ourselves as valuable citizens in front of others and to develop more self-confidence, self-respect and self-esteem, which are critical to in the process of constitution of political subjects in democratic systems. Through this interactions, autonomous subject  are constituted, as part of a continuous relational process, in which their attitude to themselves emerges in the interaction with the attitude of the other, in otherness. In this way, the relational political dimension of the individuals is manifested in the search for reinforcing the bonds of solidarity and belonging to a group. In sum, our point is that political dimension of everyday conversation should not be restricted to the argumentative potential or a solve-problem approach of formal decision oriented discussions; on the other hand, it is not make them less relevant at all. We state that civic talk should be understood based on what it can contribute to a democratic society, what is: to discover latent problems and social tendencies that affect a community; to construct shared attitudes toward political pathologies; enlarge the scope of individual concerns to embrace collective positive action. Politics, in this sense, is a way to build on a collective life, not just a fixed space or a set of rules.

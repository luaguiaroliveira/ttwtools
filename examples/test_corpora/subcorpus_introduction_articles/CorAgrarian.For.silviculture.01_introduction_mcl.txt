The global soil carbon (C) pool is considerably greater than the C pool of either vegetation or the atmosphere (Raich and Schelsinger, 1992). Soil respiration (Rs) is the primary path whereby C is released from the soil system to the atmosphere, and approximately 80 98 Pg C is emitted annually via this route (Bond Lamberty and Thomson, 2010), which is more than 10 times the annual CO2 emissions derived from fossil fuel combustion (Boden et al., 2009). Thus, even small changes in the magnitude of soil C emitted via soil respiration could have a large effect on atmospheric CO2 concentrations (Schlesinger and Andrews, 2000). Soil respiration is controlled by multiple factors, including root biomass, soil organic carbon (SOC), soil nutrient content, and microbial populations and activities. Marked changes in any of these biotic and abiotic factors could profoundly alter the global C cycle and feedbacks to climate change (Heimann and Reichstein, 2008). In the past few decades, anthropogenic activities have contributed to a marked increase in atmospheric nitrogen (N) depositions in terrestrial ecosystems and this trend is predicted to increase by 50 100 by 2030 (Galloway et al., 2008; Reay et al., 2008), with the largest increases occurring in East and South Asia (Reay et al., 2008), particularly in subtropical China (Liu et al., 2013). Nitrogen depositions can regulate forest productivity and soil microbial biomass and activities, and ultimately influence soil respiration. Most previous studies in this field have concentrated on temperate and boreal forests, which are commonly considered to be N limited ecosystems and have shown that increases in N depositions decrease soil respiration (Bond Lamberty and Thomson, 2010; Janssens et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2014). However, it has been found that soil respiration rates are most pronounced in the tropics where plant growth is luxuriant and the conditions are ideal for decomposers (Schlesinger and Andrews, 2000). Tropical and subtropical forest soils are typically rich in N and are characterized by the highest N depositions (Gao et al., 2014; Ostertag, 2001; Reay et al., 2008). However, previous studies have yielded conflicting results with regards to how increasing N depositions might affect soil respiration. A few studies that have been conducted in subtropical forests have shown that N depositions increase soil respiration in Pleioblastus amarus bamboo plantations (Tu et al., 2013) and evergreen forests (Gao et al., 2014) but reduce soil respiration in Chinese fir (Cunninghamia lanceolata) forests (Fan et al., 2014) and old growth monsoon evergreen broadleaf forests (Mo et al., 2008). Moreover, the sensitivity of soil CO2 efflux to soil temperature has also been observed to vary with N deposition rates (Mo et al., 2008; Tu et al., 2013). Consequently, at present, it is uncertain how N depositions affect soil CO2 efflux in subtropical forests, and this warrants further study.
Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis) is widely distributed in subtropical China and neighboring countries (Song et al., 2011), and in China, Moso bamboo forests, with an area of 4.43 million ha, account for 70 of the total bamboo forest area (Song et al., 2017a). Moso bamboo is well known for its rapid growth rate and can reach a mean height of 10 20m and diameter at breast height of 8 16 cm within 2 months after shoot emergence (Song et al., 2016b; Zhou et al., 2010), which contributes to a higher annual net ecosystem production (NEP) than other forest types in subtropical China (Song et al., 2017a).
Therefore, Moso bamboo forests have considerable potential with regard to playing an important role in mitigating climate change (Song et al., 2011). In our previous studies on Moso bamboo forests, we observed that N depositions increased productivity (Zhang et al., 2017b), leaf litterfall and return of N and phosphorus (P) (Zhang et al., 2017a), decomposition rates and release of N and P from leaf litter (Song et al., 2015) and fine roots (Song et al., 2017b), loss of soil dissolved organic carbon (DOC) (Lei et al., 2017), and soil microbial biomass carbon (MBC), but decreased microbial community diversity (Li et al., 2016). Additionally, in previous studies, estimates of annual soil CO2 efflux have typically been obtained by directly scaling up limited data (designated as Model 1 in the present study). These data are generally based on samples taken between 9:00 and 11:00 am on sunny days, usually at monthly and sometimes at semi monthly or weekly intervals (Hu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018). This method inherently results in large uncertainties in these estimates. It has been demonstrated that there are significant relationships between temperature and soil respiration rates, which suggests a potentially reliable approach for obtaining annual soil CO2 efflux values based on daily air temperature (designated as Model 2 in the present study) or hourly soil temperature (designated as Model 3 in the present study) data that can readily be obtained from weather stations and button thermometers, respectively. This study aimed to test the following three hypotheses: (1) N depositions stimulate soil respiration and its temperature sensitivity, (2) these stimulation effects are reduced after N saturation in the Moso bamboo forests of subtropical China and finally, (3) methods that use hourly soil temperatures to obtain estimates of annual soil CO2 effluxes are more reliable than methods based on direct upscaling.

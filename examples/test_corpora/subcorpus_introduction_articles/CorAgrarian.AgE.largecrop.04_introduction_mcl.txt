Freshwater ecosystems are extremely valuable resources and are increasingly vulnerable to anthropogenic impacts (Dudgeon et al., 2006; Lake and Bond, 2007). Effects from broad scale land use and consequent catchment modification alter the chemical, hydrological and biological cycles within streams, leading to significant impacts on stream health (Lake and Bond, 2007; Sheldon et al., 2012). These impacts include over exploitation, water pollution, flow modification, habitat degradation, species invasion (Allan, 2004; Dudgeon et al., 2006) and global environmental changes associated with climate change (Dudgeon et al., 2006). Human activities impact riverine ecosystems at multiple levels and pose a great threat to biodiversity, river health, and the ecosystem services they provide (Arthington et al., 2010; Bunn et al., 2007; Dudgeon et al., 2006). Research on riverine landscapes has occurred at varying scales, investigating the complex cause effect pathways between rivers and their physical, chemical and biological attributes (Allan, 2004; Bunn et al., 2010; Chessman and Townsend, 2010; Walsh et al., 2005).
At a regional or catchment scale, environmental factors such as climate, geology, topography and landcover shape the riverscape (Vinson and Hawkins, 1998). At the intermediate scale, fluvial and hydraulic processes form the channels, which in turn create and maintain the habitat that supports biota at the smaller scales (Allan, 2004; Snelder and Biggs, 2002). It is at the regional or catchment scale, where policy development, management and planning are often formulated and applied (Stein et al., 2002). However, the ecological evidence required to inform catchment scale is typically collected from the much smaller micro scale (Wiens, 2002). It is paramount to consider the effects of scale in river management as evidence collected at the micro scale does not always extrapolate to the larger scale and vice versa (Arnaiz et al., 2011; Wiens, 2002). In Australia, there have been many studies linking water quality and land use at the broad regional scale. Often studies investigate a region, implicating land use impacts to river health across multiple catchments in that region, or study the effects of a single land use on a macro scale (Blinn et al., 2004; Chessman and Townsend, 2010; Kay et al., 2001; Magierowski et al., 2012; Udy et al., 2006). These studies did not attempt to ascertain other possible and multiple inputs across the region and inferred the broad land use as the contributor towater quality issues. In contrast, Sheldon et al. (2012) compared sites at a catchment, riparian and reach scale within the South east Queensland region, to identify which scale had the most effect on health card scores, and thus, determine where best to concentrate restoration efforts for maximum benefits in stream health. However, it was not specified whether sites were independent of possible upstream influences not attributed to that land use category and therefore multiple cause effect pathways at the small reach scale. Sheldon et al. (2012) found that reach scale of land use had the greatest effect on water quality indicators and concluded restoration efforts would have a greater effect if undertaken in the hydrologically active region of the stream. At the macro scale, it can be more difficult to distinguish the cause effect pathways between land use and stream response which are often confounded by natural and environmental catchment characteristics (Allan, 2004; Chessman and Townsend, 2010; Magierowski et al., 2012).
Research conducted at a catchment scale, often investigates land use in general as a major contributing factor to a pollution or disturbance gradient (Li et al., 2009; Oeding and Taffs, 2015; Salomoni et al., 2011; Tan et al., 2014). Catchment scale pollution gradients range from relatively undisturbed areas in upper catchments to highly modified and degraded environments in lower catchments, where it is generally accepted that nutrient concentrations increase longitudinally, and are therefore, much higher at downstream than upstream sites (Hadwen et al., 2010). The pollution gradient is assessed using biological indicators and their related indices, such as diatoms (Blinn and Bailey, 2001; Oeding and Taffs, 2017; Philibert et al., 2006), macroinvertebrates (Chessman, 2003a; Connolly and Pearson, 2013; Newall et al., 2006), fish (Pusey and Arthington, 2003; Rose et al., 2016; Stewart Koster et al., 2011), nutrients and historic change (Logan et al., 2010; Taffs et al., 2008). The focus of these studies is understanding the relationship between environmental variables, biological indicators and river health. These types of studies do not seek to link the cause effect pathways to a single land use responsible for the inputs, rather they are more generalised in their approach, grouping all land uses together as contributing inputs to the pollution gradient and subsequent river health. At this scale, it is challenging to identify sources of negative influences on river health (Kuemmerlen et al., 2014).
There are many situations where a single land use or point source is implicated directly in influencing river health at the reach scale; forest logging (Davies and Nelson, 1994), sewage outfall (Dela Cruz et al., 2006) and urbanisation (Newall and Walsh, 2005; Sonneman et al., 2001; Walsh et al., 2001). However, there has been little attention paid to impacts of specific land uses at a microcatchment scale and how these affect water quality and stream health within a larger catchment. The aim of this study was to determine if patterns in diatom and macroinvertebrate diversity and community composition can be attributed to a specific single land use and to consider the possible implications for management within a catchment. To address this, we adopted a micro catchment approach.

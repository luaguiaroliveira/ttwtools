Most modern mobile machines are relatively safe to use as long as the operator is working inside the cabin. However, the operator has to leave the cabin every now and then during work for various reasons such as rest pauses, scheduled service of machinery, change of tools, and disturbances in work flow. Accident statistics reveal that the number of accidents while moving on access paths of mobile machinery is very high, especially in relation to the short proportion of total work time used for access and exit. According to numerous studies, 17 50 of accidents associated with the use of mobile machinery occur while moving on the access pathsa majority of the accidents take place while leaving the cabin (Suutarinen, 1997; Backstro m andA ˚ berg, 1998).With commercial vehicles, Nicholson and David (1985) have reported the share of accidents during ingress and egress to fall in the same range. Modern safety cabins decrease operators’ risk of falling and being crushed under the machine in mobile machine overturning accidents, but they also make the access paths in many cases more complicated. It seems that access path accidents have not decreased after safety cabins became a required part of the mobile machine, during recent decades (Health and Safety Executive ref. The sequences of events leading to underfoot accidents in general have been studied by e.g. Gro nqvist (1995), Jackson and Cohen (1995) and Nagata (1991). The most important causes for falling are dimensional inconsistency inherent in some stairways, and too small a friction between the foot and the surface material. Both the lowfrictio n and rapid changes in friction are risk factors. Ojanen (1991) found that the access path of a tractor is seldom used properlyonly in 6 of egresses. Common problems are shortcuts in the use of steps and handrails, jumping and a twisted back. The motor performance in the access path use is most often an automatic, skill based activity as a result of frequent operation (Hammer, 1991). It has been stated that the more automated a certain operation is, the more prone it is to disturbances (Hacker, 1982). The footedness is another cause of variability possibly causing risks in access paths if not taken into account (Va yrynen et al., 1996). The usability of access paths, and the variability of human action (between and among users), and the interaction between the two have a role in sequences leading to accidents that must be studied further. A variability free human performance should not be expectedeven if the variability is seen as an internal or external ’’error’’ or erroneus action (Hollnagel, 1998). If expected, the design may create latent opportunities for failures. Accidental consequences of the variability in human action reveal a mismatch in human machine interaction.

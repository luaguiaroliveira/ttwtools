Soil water is an important component of the hydrological processes in forest ecosystems and serves as a link to rainfall, surface water, and groundwater (Brooks et al., 2010; Orlowski et al., 2016; Jia et al., 2017). Soil Water also plays a critical role in the processes of evapotranspiration, infiltration, runoff, and erosion (Evaristo et al., 2015; Gispert et al., 2017). The spatial temporal pattern of soil water is influenced by many factors, among which rainfall is the primary factor (Shellito et al., 2016). The frequency and intensity of rainfall directly affect the magnitudes and dynamics of soil water (Verma et al., 2011; Lai et al., 2016). However, rainfall impacted soil water could be further regulated by vegetation including forests, shrubs and grasslands, where forests have the greatest effects on soil water through increasing leaf interception and root water uptake (Gautam et al., 2017; Metzger et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018). Riparian forests, which typically suffer from frequent flooding, are more capable of withstanding floods than inland forests (An et al., 2007). Hence, riparian forests could exert an important role in mitigating flood, especially under conditions of global climate change that cause wet areas to be wetter with associated frequent floods (Sherwood and Fu, 2014). Therefore, exploring the response mechanism of soil water in riparian forests to rainfall events is critical to understand the relationship between forest ecosystems and hydrological processes, and provides powerful measures for extreme rainfall events.
Despite its importance, our understanding of the response of forest soil water to rainfall events is still limited, which is mainly reflected in the following two aspects. First, most previous studies focused on the response of soil water to single rainfall events (Keesstra et al., 2016; Lv et al., 2016), while research on the response of soil water to different magnitudes of rainfall is scarce. Although a few studies on the response of soil water to different amounts of rainfall have been conducted, their observations remain controversial. For instance, Lozano Parra et al. (2015) reported that soil moisture increased gradually with the increase of precipitation in tree woodland of Spain. In contrast, a recent study conducted in Spain found that there was no significant difference in soil moisture between two magnitudes of rainfall (Gispert et al., 2017). Hence, more studies are needed to explore how forest soil water responds to rainfall. Furthermore, the abovementioned studies mainly focused on pure stands, and the comparative studies between pure and mixed stands remain unavailable. Recent studies indicated that the soil water repellency in mixed stands was significantly lower than that in pure stands (Alanis et al., 2017), indirectly reflecting the higher soil water holding capacity potential of mixed stands. Nevertheless, direct evidence that mixed stands contain higher soil water holding capacity than pure stands is still lacking. Overall, the response of soil water to different magnitudes of rainfall between pure and mixed stands needs to be explored further. Second, it remains obscured which factors mainly affect the spatial temporal pattern of soil water in riparian forests. Specifically, soil properties (Gwak and Kim, 2017), vegetation types (Korres et al., 2015), topography (Rosenbaum et al., 2012) and climate (Deng et al., 2016) could impact the soil water dynamics. Soil and vegetation characteristics are generally considered two critical factors that influence soil water dynamics when in a specific area that contains a similar topography and climate (Korres et al., 2015). For instance, soil properties, including particulate matter and texture, were the dominant factors that impact soil water dynamics under different vegetation types (Fang et al., 2016; Gwak and Kim, 2017). However, Dick et al. (2018) illustrated that vegetation distribution and the canopy structure were the dominant factors affecting soil water dynamics. A similar observation was also reported by Korres et al. (2015) and Liu and Shao (2016), suggesting that vegetation type was the main factor that caused soil water temporal dynamics and profile characteristics. These studies demonstrate that dominant factors affecting soil water in different vegetation types greatly differ at the regional scales. Further studies are therefore needed to examine what factors regulate the distribution of soil water in different stand types of riparian forests. 
The Middle lower reaches of the Yangtze River are some of the typical areas dominated by riparian forests. Under climate warming, floods have occurred frequently in this region during the past several decades. To alleviate the impacts of extreme climate, a large area of afforestation has been conducted along the Yangtze River over the past decades, most of which were pure stands of Populus deltoides. Previous studies reported that pure stands have a lower stress tolerance than mixed stands (Pretzsch et al., 2013; Thurm et al., 2016), but it is still unclear whether or not rainfall impacted soil water dynamics in mixed stands differ from that of pure stands, and which kind of stand contains a higher water holding capacity. In this study, we used stable hydrogen isotope to examine the contribution of rainfall to soil water (CRSW) in two stand types of riparian forests, the pure stand of P. deltoides and the mixed stand of P. deltoides Taxodium ascendens Liquidambar formosana, under three different magnitudes of rainfall events. We further measured the soil properties, litter biomass, aboveground and underground biomasses to reveal which factor was the best predictor in regulating the CRSW. This study mainly aims to test the following two hypotheses: (1) The CRSW in the mixed stand was greater than that in the pure stand responding to different magnitudes of rainfall. (2) Vegetation rather than soil was the primary factor affecting the CRSW.

Recent climate models project that droughts are predicted to increase in duration, intensity, and geographic coverage (Dai, 2011).
Concurrently, extreme precipitation events occur frequently across the world (Bytnerowicz et al., 2007; Werner and Egbert, 2009). The increased drying wetting alterations affect terrestrial carbon (C) cycling, particularly for soil, which comprises the Earth’s largest terrestrial C reservoir (Reichstein et al., 2013). Hence, there is an increased interest in quantifying the soil respiration (SR) response to drought and extreme precipitation event (Shen et al., 2016).
Drought is a major natural hazard that is characterized by below average precipitation over a long period of time (Xu et al., 2015). Droughts occur over most parts of the world, even in humid regions (Dai, 2011). Due to the monsoon climate interacted with the complicated geographical landscapes, many parts of China have suffered from serious droughts in the past 50 years (Xu et al., 2015), which have greatly influenced terrestrial C cycling. Moreover, strong spatial and temporal variations of drought have been reported in China. For example, Wu et al. (2011) quantified drought in China during 1950 2006 and found that Central and Northeastern China had substantial drying trend. In addition, the drought frequency was higher in spring and winter across China (Yu et al., 2014). Understanding the influences of seasonal drought and extreme precipitation events on SR is of primary importance for terrestrial C cycling assessment under scenarios of altered precipitation patterns (Falge et al., 2002; Reth et al., 2005; Xie et al., 2014).
The drying wetting cycles due to isolated precipitation events can produce pulsed inputs of moisture, resulting in pulse response CO2 efflux characteristic that is known as “Birch effect” (Birch, 1964). The pulse magnitude of SR following rewetting of dry soil has been attributed to numerous biotic and abiotic factors, such as pore connectivity (Smith et al., 2017), labile soil organic C (Werner and Egbert, 2009), microbial activity (Blazewicz et al., 2014), accumulated dead microbial biomass over dry periods (Bottner, 1985; Sponseller, 2007), and carbonate dissolution (Rey, 2015). Despite the increasing number of studies and recent advances in our understanding of precipitation pulses, several aspects still need urgent attention. For example, how important is soil microclimate (temperature and moisture) prior to precipitation event in controlling the response of SR to precipitation events remains unclear. Soil respiration rate varies with soil temperature and moisture (Qi and Xu, 2001; Reth et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2017). Initial soil temperature and moisture directly regulate root activity (Ford et al., 2012), microbial metabolism (Denef et al., 2001), and availability of decomposable organic substrate (Huxman et al., 2004a), thus could have profound impacts on the response of pulse effect. In the Sonoran Desert, for example, the pulse response of SR is intensified by the dry condition of the antecedent soil, but the effect is less obvious if the soil is wet before the precipitation event (Cable et al., 2008). To our knowledge, however, few studies have quantified the effect of antecedent moisture on the response of SR to precipitation events in forest ecosystems.
The Qinling Mountain Huai River range has been recognized as north south demarcation zone in China, due to remarkable differences in climate, vegetation, soil, and custom between two sides of the zone. The total area of the demarcation zone is approximately 145,500.74 km2, with a total length of 1666.28 km from east to west (Zhang et al., 2012), which is also known as a temperate subtropics transitional zone in China. The forest in this region is characterized by high biodiversity and complex stand structure. Climate zones at middle northern latitudes as well as climatic transitional zones are the ones most sensitive to climate change (Mahlstein et al., 2013). Therefore, it has been suggested that forest ecosystems in climate transitional zone will play progressively important roles in the terrestrial C cycle. Although a rapid increase of SR following precipitation event has been reported in a variety of ecosystems (Davidson et al., 2000; Sponseller, 2007; Shi et al., 2011; Ma et al., 2012), only a few studies have analyzed the effect of episodic precipitation on different components of SR [autotrophic respiration (AR) and heterotrophic respiration (HR)]. Previous studies suggest that responses of AR and HR to precipitation event differ due to the differences in substrate supply and sensitivity to soil moisture (Huxman et al., 2004b; Sponseller, 2007).
For example, in the Sonoran Desert, autotrophic (plant) respiration was a significant (60 ) source of respiration and was more sensitive to rainfall pulses (Cable et al., 2008). To date, information on the responses of SR components to precipitation is still rather limited, especially in forest ecosystem. Although water availability is not a limiting factor in most forest ecosystems, discrete rainfall events can also trigger a rapid pulse of soil respiration (Shi et al., 2011). The cumulative triggered pulse of CO2 from forests is likely contributing more to atmospheric CO2 concentration due to its greater substrate availability and higher occurrence of precipitation events compared to those in arid and semiarid landscapes (Song et al., 2016).
In this study, short term responses of SR and HR to spring drought and precipitation events are investigated in an oak forest (Quercus variabilis BI.) in Central China. The objectives of the present study are to (1) examine the effects of precipitation events on SR and its components, (2) investigate the effects of different spring drought duration on the responses of SR and its components to precipitation events, and (3) compare the differences of precipitation induced increase in total soil C loss between different drought intensities.

Global average surface temperature has risen by 0.85 C over the period from 1883 to 2012 and is still increasing (IPCC, 2014) with a heterogeneous pattern across the world. More specifically, the boreal ecosystem will experience the greatest warming among forest biomes (Bronson et al., 2009). Such a magnitude of change in climate may require humans to influence plant distribution through artificial migrations of species or provenances to match the dynamics of forest ecosystems with the future climate, thus maintaining optimal forest health and the long term persistence of harvestable stock (Schwartz et al., 2012).
Considering the circumpolar boreal forest, artificial regeneration is applied to 50 90 of the total harvested area (Esseen et al., 1997; Leinonen et al., 2008). In Canada, 55 of harvested stands are artificially regenerated (Natural Resources Canada, 2018). Plantation provides greater control over tree species composition and forest productivity, allowing forest management objectives to be met according to specific local environmental conditions. However, while some species have a uniform climatic tolerance over their native range, others could exhibit local populations with specific responses that reflect the different geographical climatic conditions.
Trees have developed mechanisms to synchronise timings of growth and reproduction with favourable climatic and environmental conditions (Korner and Basler, 2010). Phenological traits are important aspects of plant adaptation that defines the seasonal timings of biological events, ensuring optimal growth while minimizing the risks of damage exhibiting large changes as a response to climate variations. Indeed, several monitoring networks demonstrate the occurrence of phenological shifts from the mid 20th century onwards following the ongoing climate change in Europe (Chen et al., 2018b; Menzel et al., 2006), Asia (Chen, 2013), North America (Boulouf Lugo et al., 2012) and South and Central America (Morellato et al., 2013). Earlier growth reactivation leads to lengthening of the growing season (Boulouf Lugo et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2017), although this observation is not confirmed by Chen et al. (2018a). A longer growing season could virtually enhance carbon uptake and net ecosystem productivity of forests (Randerson et al., 1999). However, an earlier bud burst increases the risk of late frosts in spring (Ma et al., 2018), while lengthening of the growing season increases the risk of early frosts in autumn (Bennie et al., 2010). If trees are not able to match, or rematch, their phenology with the new climatic conditions, fitness and growth performance of local populations could be dramatically affected.
Dormancy is an adaptation mechanism evolved in seasonal climates to survive harsh winter conditions (Charrier et al., 2015). At the end of growing season, plants develop dormant buds and enter dormancy (Hanninen, 1990). Warm spring temperatures (forcing), increasing day length (photoperiod), and length and intensity of winter temperatures (chilling) lead growth reactivation of the apical meristems in the spring (Flynn and Wolkovich, 2018). It is evident that temperature has a central and leading role in this process, affecting both timing and rates of the successive phenological phases (Badeck et al., 2004; Rossi and Isabel, 2017; Salminen and Jalkanen, 2005).
Populations of species with wide geographical distributions can exhibit adaptations to local environmental conditions that result from a close interaction between climate and genotype. The presence of such populations, called ecotypes, may indicate functional differences across the species range, potentially leading to diverging responses to weather conditions, and, consequently, to a changing climate (Rossi and Isabel, 2017). Changes in bud phenology are well known, with different gene polymorphisms implicated in genetic variability (Rossi, 2015). Divergent timings of bud phenology also correspond to different cambial activity dynamics and, in turn, wood growth (Perrin et al., 2017). Within a context of a growing interest in artificial forest regeneration, an understanding of the phenology across the ecotypes of tree species can be a tool for suitable seed transfers in assisted migration (Beaulieu et al., 2004).
Spring phenology and its responses to climate change are well documented in the literature (Wolkovich et al., 2012). However, autumn phenology has received less attention in both natural and controlled environments and still remains partly unexplored (Chen et al., 2018a; Gallinat et al., 2015). The aim of this study was to provide a better understanding of the temporal boundaries of primary growth by investigating the timings of bud burst and bud set in five populations of black spruce [Picea mariana (Mill.) B.S.P] growing in a common garden. This species has a wide transcontinental boreal distribution in North America, covering the latitudinal range from Alaska to Labrador. Based on a clinal variation in spring phenology, with delayed onset observed in ecotypes from warmer sites of origin (Rossi, 2015), three hypotheses were tested for autumn phenology (Fig. 1): (A) bud set occurs at the same time for all ecotypes, leading to a shorter season for those from warmer sites of origin, suggesting a response to photoperiod; (B) bud set occurs earlier in ecotypes from warmer sites of origin, leading to shorter growing seasons, suggesting a response to temperature; (C) bud set occurs later in ecotypes from warmer sites of origin, leading to similar length of growing seasons among ecotypes, suggesting that growth cessation is pre determined and controlled by adaptive mechanisms.

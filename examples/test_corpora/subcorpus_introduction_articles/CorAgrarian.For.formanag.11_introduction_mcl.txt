One of the most significant landscape elements in Central Europe are forests. In Germany, approximately 31 of the surface is covered by mainly managed forests. Natural or unmanaged forests exist only in fragments, comprising less than 3 of total forest area (BMELV, 2014). Ongoing efforts to increase the proportion of green energy focus on additional harvest of wood as well meant strategy to counteract climate change (Schulze et al., 2012) act as additional threat for forest ecosystems because often these short rotation plantations are mainly composed of fast growing tree species which are often untypical for the sites where they are cultivated. Therefore, ecologically sustainable and conservation compatible practices in managed forests, such as integrative multifunctional forest management programs (e.g. Borrass et al., 2017), as well as the abandonment of formerly managed forests gain importance for the conservation of biodiversity in the European landscape. Moreover, Ammer et al. (in press) highlighted the importance of understanding the relationships between different components of management, forest structure and biodiversity. This demands the solid investigation of environmental, structural and forest management variables to test their effects on biodiversity. Usually, studies investigating management effects on biodiversity comprise species groups such as mammals, birds, beetles or higher plants whereas equally important forest organisms such as cryptogam plants are strongly underrepresented (but see Paillet et al., 2010; Boch et al., 2013a,b).
Among cryptogams, bryophytes constitute an important and permanent component of the forest flora and diversity. They colonize various substrates, which are unsuitable for vascular plants, because of low light intensity or low nutrient level, such as deadwood, bark, rocks, and open soil (Smith, 1982; Bates and Farmer, 1992). They provide shelter habitats, food, and nest material for many animals (Gerson, 1982; Boch et al., 2013c). Due to their high abundance in forests they are considerable primary producers of biomass, conduct photosynthesis around the year, and are therefore influencing the carbon and nutrient cycles (e.g. Turetsky, 2003).
In forests, different ecological guilds of bryophytes can be distinguished by the substrate on which they are growing, including terricolous, lignicolous, corticolous and saxicolous species that occur on soil, deadwood, bark of living trees and shrubs, or rocks, respectively.
As diversity and quality of these substrates is affected by forest management, bryophytes are suitable indicators for the effect of management on forest conditions (Rose, 1992). Especially typical woodland bryophytes, which are strictly depending on forest conditions (Rose, 1999; Norden et al., 2007; Preussing et al., 2011; Schmidt et al., 2011; Kriebitzsch et al., 2013, Molder et al., 2015), are valuable indicator organisms to estimate the naturalness and integrity of forest stands (Frego, 2007).
Some studies dealt with forest management effects on bryophytes and demonstrated their sensitivity to management practices. However, comparative studies on bryophyte diversity in forest ecosystems were mostly restricted to boreal regions and only compared unmanaged with managed forests (e.g. Andersson and Hytteborn, 1991; Frisvoll and Presto, 1997; Ross Davis and Frego, 2002; Nelson and Halpern, 2005; Vellak and Ingerpuu, 2005; reviewed in Paillet et al., 2010). The studies from temperate European forests were restricted to only one study region and one ecological guild such as epiphytes (Friedel et al., 2006; Mežaka and Znotiņa, 2006; Bardat and Aubert, 2007; Fritz et al., 2008, 2009a, 2009b; Kiraly and odor, 2010) or epixylic species (Andersson and Hytteborn, 1991; odor et al., 2006) or incorporated only one forest type (Marialigeti et al., 2009; Horvat et al., 2017; Schall et al., 2018). Thus, a comprehensive assessment how different groups of bryophytes respond to a range of management regimes across different regions is still lacking.
In addition, the relationship between the diversities of bryophytes and vascular plants has only rarely been studied in forests (but see Ingerpuu et al., 2003), and it is unclear whether management affects both groups similarly. In grasslands, bryophyte diversity is a very good indicator not only for the total vascular plant diversity but also for the diversities of many individual plant and animal taxa. In fact, bryophyte diversity showed the strongest relation with of the overall diversity of grasslands (Manning et al., 2015 referring to multidiversity sensu Allan et al., 2014). In forests, the richness of terricolous bryophyte and herb species seems to be positively related as well (Marialigeti et al., 2009). However, it remained open whether particular forest conditions affect the presence of typical woodland indicator species of both bryophytes and vascular plants in the same way. We therefore conducted a large scale investigation on the relationship between forest management and bryophyte species richness across a broad range of management types in three regions in Germany, allowing to generalize our findings for temperate, lowland to montane forests in Central Europe. We included bryophytes of all ecological guilds and analysed management responses of each ecological guild, of the group of woodland indicator bryophyte species and of all bryophyte species together. Furthermore, to test the accordance of management responses on the vegetation, we studied the relationship between species richness of bryophytes and herbaceous vascular plants on the forest floor.
Our main questions are:
(1) How does the species richness of all bryophytes and of woodland indicator bryophyte species respond to forest management?
(2) How does bryophyte species richness respond to variation in environmental variables?
(3) How is the richness of bryophytes and herbaceous vascular plants related?
